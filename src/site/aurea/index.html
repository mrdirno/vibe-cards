Origami Wrist Canopy Bracer

A bracer you cut flat from one sheet and lock together with tabs. It wraps the wrist, and its pleated canopy opens into a fan that shades your arm. No glue. No hardware.

Schematic, not the panel map. The sheet is the authority.

What it is

Twelve flat panels, cut at full size from one A2 sheet, that lock into each other with tabs and become a curved band around your wrist. Above the band the panels are pleated, so the whole thing folds shut against your forearm and opens into a fan when you want shade. Nothing is glued and nothing is screwed — the geometry is the fastener.

It weighs about 78 g finished. It is a wearable and it is also a lesson in pleating: flat until the last tab seats, and then it holds a curve.

How to build it

AN AFTERNOON. LONGER IF YOU GLUE IT, WHICH YOU DO NOT HAVE TO.

Print the template at true scale

The sheet is drawn 1:1 in millimetres on A2, 420 × 594 mm. Print at 100% or “Actual size”. No home printer takes A2, so tile it across A4 or Letter and trim the overlap — the tiles line up because the drawing is dimensioned rather than fitted.

“Fit to page” is the one setting that ruins this. It shrinks the sheet by a few percent, which is invisible on paper and fatal in the joints: at 97% a 12 mm tab is 11.6 mm and every one of them is loose.

Check the ruler before you cut anything

There is a 10 cm calibration ruler printed on the sheet for exactly this moment. Put a real ruler against it. It has to measure 100 mm. If it does not, the print scale is wrong — change the setting and print again. Thirty seconds against a whole sheet of material and an afternoon.

Cut the solid black lines through

Solid black is a cut. Take the blade all the way through, one panel at a time, and keep the offcuts until the dry-fit is done — a mis-cut tab can be patched from a scrap of the same sheet.

Score the red dashed lines — before you fold, not after

Dashed red is a fold. Score it about halfway through on one face, then valley-fold it 180° so the two faces meet. The drawing says score then fold, in that order, and it matters: an unscored fold in 2 mm stock bulges, and every bulge fights the tabs for the rest of the build.

Keep the grain arrows pointing the same way

The arrows are grain direction, and they run lengthwise so the canopy flexes where it is meant to. Board folds cleanly along the grain and cracks across it. Lay every panel out with its arrow the same way before you cut.

Match the circled numbers, edge to edge

Each edge carries a circled index. 1 goes to 1, 2 goes to 2. Every edge has exactly one partner, so a number left over means a panel is the wrong way round rather than missing.

Assemble clockwise, P01 through P12

Slide each 12 mm tab into its 12.4 mm slot and work clockwise around the cuff. The order matters: later panels close over earlier joins, and a ring assembled out of sequence leaves a tab you can no longer reach.

TAB12.0
SLOT12.4

0.4 mm of clearance is the whole fit: tight enough to hold under its own friction, loose enough to seat with your fingers. If a tab will not go in, the fold above it is not fully closed — work the fold, not the tab.

Glue only if you want it permanent

It holds dry. That is the design. Adhesive is a decision you make after the whole bracer has been dry-fitted and worn once, because glue is the step with no undo and the dry-fit is where you find out a panel is mirrored. Contact cement on laminate, PVA on board — and never on a fold.

The card

Where the images come from

The editorial image is a synthetic render. The arm on the card front was made by an image model. It shows a forearm and a hand and nothing else — no face, and no real person. You cannot tell that by looking, which is the entire reason it is written here on the page a stranger reads rather than only in a metadata file beside it.

The blueprint is not editorial. It is the fabrication template — drawing ORI-WCB-012, rev 01: 1:1, in millimetres, cut lines, fold lines, index numbers, tabs and slots. If the render and the sheet ever disagree about this object, the sheet is right. The render is a picture of it; the sheet is the thing.
